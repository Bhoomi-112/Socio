What if everyone is pretending?

A simple guide to pluralistic ignorance — the social psychology phenomenon where people privately think or feel one thing, but mistakenly believe everyone else thinks something different.

So, what is it?

Pluralistic ignorance happens when members of a group misread one another's private attitudes. Many people may privately disagree with a behaviour or norm, yet each person assumes that most others accept it.

In plain English: “I don't really agree with this, but everyone else seems fine with it — so maybe I'm the only one.”

It is a group-level pattern, not simply one person being confused. The shared misperception can make a norm look more widely accepted than it really is.

Picture this

The silent classroom

A lecturer explains a difficult topic. Several students are confused, but nobody asks a question. Each student looks around, sees that nobody else is speaking, and assumes everyone else understands. They stay quiet too.

What's really happening? The silence is being mistaken for understanding. If students could see how many others were also confused, the room might feel very different.

This classroom example is discussed in Miller and McFarland's work on pluralistic ignorance.

How does it happen?

1
We look for social cues.
When we are unsure what is normal, we watch what other people say and do.
2
We see only the public version.
People may hide uncertainty or disagreement to avoid embarrassment, judgement, or standing out.
3
We mistake silence for agreement.
Because others appear comfortable or stay quiet, we infer that they privately agree.
4
The mistaken norm reinforces itself.
People continue acting as if they agree, making the original assumption look true.

Research also points to factors such as vocal minorities, social identity, group dynamics, and unrepresentative media visibility.

Where might you notice it?

At college

Students may think they are the only ones who find a lecture confusing or disagree with a campus norm.

With friends

Someone may go along with a plan because they assume everyone else genuinely wants it.

At work

Employees may stay quiet about a concern because they assume colleagues or managers support the current approach.

Online

Loud or highly visible opinions can make a minority view seem more common than it is.

These are illustrative situations, not proof that pluralistic ignorance is occurring in every such case.

How can we challenge it?

1
Ask, don't assume.
Use open, non-judgemental questions instead of guessing what the group thinks.
2
Make disagreement safe.
Invite different views and make it clear that people can speak without being mocked or punished.
3
Look for better evidence.
Don't treat a few loud voices, likes, comments, or a quiet room as a reliable survey of everyone's beliefs.
4
Share accurate group information.
When reliable, representative information about people's actual views is available, it can correct mistaken assumptions.

These are practical ways to question a perceived norm, not a guaranteed intervention for every group or situation.

Organises research on the factors that can lead to pluralistic ignorance and its consequences for people's attitudes, behaviour, and organisations. Its framework identifies seven possible social-comparison errors.
